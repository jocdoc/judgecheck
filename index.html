    How JudgeCheck's Statistics Work

    A plain-language explanation of what the flags, lists, and reports on this site actually mean.

    The Core Idea

    JudgeCheck never asks “was this score high or low?” It asks: did one judge score noticeably differently than the other judges on the same panel, for the same dancers, in the same round? Every check compares a judge to their fellow judges sitting right next to them — not to some outside standard.

    Individual Event Reports: Clear / Second Look / Flagged

    For one competition round, JudgeCheck checks whether any judge's scores drifted unusually far from the rest of the panel's consensus. To decide what counts as “unusual” rather than normal disagreement between judges, it runs a computer simulation: it randomly reshuffles which judge gave which score, thousands of times, and sees how often a mismatch this large happens by pure chance. If the real result is rarer than chance alone would produce, that round gets flagged.

    Clear — nothing statistically surprising.  Second Look — a noticeable but not extreme mismatch.  Flagged — very unlikely to be a coincidence.

    Favoritism / Punishment Patterns

    When the same dancer or same school is scored by the same judge across multiple, separate competitions, JudgeCheck checks for a pattern: does this judge consistently score them higher (“favored”) or lower (“punished”) than the rest of the panel, over and over? One odd result can be a fluke — a repeated pattern across separate events is more telling.

    Correcting for “Testing Everyone”

    JudgeCheck is checking many judges against many dancers and schools at once. Run enough comparisons, and a few will look surprising purely by chance — the same way flipping a coin a thousand times will occasionally produce ten heads in a row. A standard statistical correction (Benjamini-Hochberg) adjusts the bar for “flagged” based on how many total comparisons were run, so the flagged list stays trustworthy even at large scale.

    The Two Landing-Page Lists

    Highest Flag Rate — Of every event a judge worked, what share came back Second Look or Flagged? Answers: how often is this judge under any suspicion at all?

    Least Consistent — Averages how strong the evidence was across just that judge's own flagged/second-look events. A judge can rank high here without ever having one severe incident — instead showing a repeated, milder pattern across many events.

    What This Is — and Isn't

    Every result is statistical: it flags “unlikely to be random chance,” not “this judge did something wrong.” Legitimate explanations can produce a flag too — a judge who specializes in a particular style, an unusual panel, or plain coincidence at low probability. Flags are meant to prompt a closer human look, not serve as a verdict.

    For a Statistical Audience (very brief)

    Single-event check: test statistic is the most extreme judge-vs-panel-mean deviation among competitors in a round; significance is calibrated empirically via permutation (reshuffling judge labels within the round) rather than a parametric null, since panel size per round is small. Cross-event checks pool a judge's gap-vs-panel-mean across every shared event per competitor/school and run a one-sample t-test against zero. The two landing-page lists are aggregations over that same already-corrected output (notable-event rate; mean q restricted to non-clear events) — not additional inferential tests. Multiplicity is controlled via Benjamini-Hochberg FDR across all simultaneously tested judge-subject pairs (q < 0.10 flagged, < 0.05 for event-level severity). No correction is applied across the three test families (single-event, competitor-level, team-level) or across repeated archive-wide re-tests over time, so the FDR bound holds within each family, not globally.

    JudgeCheck is a statistical screening tool, not proof of misconduct. It measures how unusual a scoring pattern is compared to chance; it cannot know why a pattern exists. Results should prompt review by the appropriate governing body — never punishment on their own. Uploaded files are processed in memory and are not stored, unless you explicitly choose "Save to archive" after reviewing the extracted data — that's the only action that saves anything permanently. PDF extraction can make mistakes — always check the preview table against your source document before running the analysis or saving it.
